Settings, framing, light, and sound. The habits that make every clip usable.

The Basics01Start here

Core rules

A few principles that apply to every clip, every location, every trip. If you only read one section, make it this one.

Shoot vertical. Always.

Reels on Instagram and Facebook are 9:16, which means portrait mode on your phone. Horizontal video is unusable on these platforms. When in doubt: phone upright, camera rolling.

This is the one rule that overrides everything else. Every other mistake is fixable in editing. This one isn't.

Give every clip a hook

People decide whether to keep watching in the first second or two. Something needs to be happening right away: a pour, a door opening, a wave breaking, someone turning toward a view. Start recording a few seconds before the moment so the editor can open on it.

Ask yourself: what is the one thing in this clip that would make someone stop scrolling? Make sure it's in there, and make sure it happens early.

Then ask a second question: would someone send this to the person they travel with? Instagram has said that how long people watch and how often they share a video to a friend are among the biggest signals it uses to show it to new people.

Something should always move

A perfectly still, empty frame reads as a photo, and photos get scrolled past in a video feed. Hold the camera still, but find a shot where something inside it moves: water, steam, flags, a curtain, a person walking through, light shifting across a wall.

One clip, one moment

The strongest clips focus on a single idea, space, or view. Don't try to capture everything in one shot. A slow pan across a dining room is one moment. A view from a balcony is one moment. Keep them separate and let each one breathe.

Clip length

The default is 30 to 60 seconds. Long, continuous clips are the goal. They capture atmosphere, give the editing team flexibility, and often don't require anything to be stitched together. One great 45-second clip of a lobby, a view, or a dining room can stand on its own as a Reel.

Shorter clips of 10 to 15 seconds have their place for specific details (food interaction, hands on an object, a single gesture), but they're the exception, not the approach. When in doubt, keep rolling.

The floor, not the goal. Even a short detail clip needs at least 8 seconds. Start rolling a beat before the moment and keep rolling a beat after it. Editors call that extra footage "handles," and they need it on both ends to cut cleanly. A 2-second clip is almost unusable.

Get the wide, the medium, and the close

For anything worth filming, grab three versions: a wide shot that shows where you are, a medium shot of the main subject, and a close-up of a detail. Editors cut between them to set the pace, and the change in shot size is what keeps eyes on screen.

Slow down

Move at half your normal pace. Slow movement reads as intentional. Fast movement reads as nervous. If you're panning, do it once, deliberately. If you're walking, imagine you're carrying a full glass of water.

Film what clients ask about

Pretty scenery is everywhere. What travel accounts are seeing work better right now is the useful stuff and a bit of personality. You know what your clients ask, so film the answer:

  • How big the room or cabin really is, shot from the doorway
  • The walk from the room to the pool, the beach, or the restaurant
  • The shower, the closet, the view from a standard room, not just the suite
  • How the tender, the transfer, or the check-in actually works
Say one useful thing to camera if you're comfortable: "Ask for a room on this side, you get the sunset." A real tip from someone who's been there is the kind of thing people save and send.

Only send what you film

Instagram now limits how far accounts reach when they mostly repost other people's videos. A hotel's promo clip or a video saved from someone else's social won't help us, even with credit. Your own footage, however imperfect, is what we can use.

Think in moving photographs

Many clips will have music or voiceover added in editing. Think of each one as a moving photograph: a clean, calm visual the marketing team can narrate or score. Your job is to capture the moment. Their job is to make it sing.

Phone setup

Do this before you arrive at a location. Fumbling with settings mid-shoot costs you moments.

Set up your phone in two minutes

You only do this once. Pick your phone, then tap Next to go through it step by step. The circle on each picture shows exactly where to tap.

Every phone is a little different. Depending on your phone model and software version, some settings may be missing, have a slightly different name, or sit in a different spot. If a step doesn't match your screen, look for the closest option or skip it. You can't break anything by trying.

Keep readingResolution & frame rate · Stabilization · Grid overlay

Resolution & frame rate

  • Set resolution to 4K if available, 1080p minimum. Higher resolution gives editors room to crop, stabilize, and reframe without losing quality.
  • Shoot at 30 frames per second (fps) for standard footage. Use 60fps for anything involving movement: water, people walking, activities. It looks smoother and gives editors a slow-motion option.

Stabilization

  • Turn on your phone's built-in stabilization. Depending on the phone it may be called "Stabilization," "Steady," or "Action mode." Many phones also have optical stabilization (OIS) built into the lens, which is always on.
  • If stabilization isn't available, move slowly and deliberately. Shaky footage is the single biggest quality killer.
Cinematic mode on iPhone is not stabilization. It softly blurs the background so the subject stands out, which looks polished on slow, atmospheric clips. Swipe to Cinematic in the camera app, then tap record.

Grid overlay

Turn on the grid overlay in your camera settings. It helps you frame shots using the rule of thirds and keeps your horizons straight.

The Basics03

Framing & composition

You don't need to be a photographer to frame a good shot. These principles apply to everything.

Rule of thirds

Imagine your screen divided into a 3×3 grid. Place your main subject (a building, a horizon line, a focal point) along one of those grid lines rather than dead centre. It looks more natural and gives the frame room to breathe.

Tip: Turn on the grid overlay in your camera settings. You'll immediately start framing better shots.
Keep readingPick a lens, don't pinch · Foreground interest · Leave headroom · Shoot from the guest's eyes · Keep the good stuff in the middle · Anchor the viewer

Pick a lens, don't pinch

Your phone has a few real lenses, shown as buttons like 0.5, 1x, and on some phones 2, 3, or 5. Tap one of those instead of pinching the screen. Pinching between them just crops the picture and makes it softer.

  • 1x is your sharpest, best lens, especially in dim light. Use it by default.
  • 0.5 (ultra-wide) is great for small rooms, cabins, and big views. It gets grainy in low light, so save it for daylight.
  • The longer lenses are good for details from a distance, like a dish across the table or a facade across the square.

Foreground interest

The best travel footage has something in the foreground, like a cocktail on a railing, palm fronds, or a poolside chair, with the destination visible behind it. It creates depth and puts the viewer in the scene rather than outside looking at a postcard.

Leave headroom

Don't crop off the tops of things. Leave space above subjects, buildings, and landmarks. Tight crops that cut off heads or rooflines look amateurish and give editors nothing to work with.

Shoot from the guest's eyes

Film from where a traveller would actually be: eye level when walking in, seated height at a table, lying down on a lounger looking out. Point-of-view footage lets the viewer imagine themselves there, which is the whole job of travel content.

Keep the good stuff in the middle

On Reels, the caption, username, and buttons sit over the bottom third and the right edge of the screen. Keep faces, focal points, and anything important in the centre and upper middle of the frame so nothing gets covered.

Anchor the viewer

Abstract close-ups can be beautiful, but without context the viewer doesn't know where they are. Include elements that anchor them to a real place: architecture, a view, signage, or people moving naturally through the scene.

The Basics04

Camera movement

When you move the camera, move with intention. When you hold still, hold completely still.

Moves that work

  • Slow pan: left to right or right to left across a scene, once, deliberately
  • Walking reveal: moving toward or around a subject so it gradually comes into frame
  • Tilt up: starting at ground level or a detail and slowly tilting up to reveal the full scene
  • Drift past: walking slowly alongside something at arm's length, keeping it in frame
  • Stillness: holding a shot completely still while the scene moves within it (water, people, light)
Keep readingMoves to avoid

Moves to avoid

  • Zooming in or out while recording. Move your feet instead.
  • Fast whip pans
  • Reframing mid-clip. If you need a different angle, stop and start a new clip.
  • Spinning or circling, which is disorienting and hard to edit
End on something. Every move should finish on a view, a detail, or a person worth looking at. Then hold there for a few seconds before you stop.

The Basics05

Lighting

Lighting is the single biggest factor in whether footage looks professional. The best light is free and comes from the sky.

Golden hour

The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset. Light is warm, soft, and directional. If you have schedule flexibility, plan your best shots around these windows. The difference is significant.

Keep readingOvercast days · Midday sun · Indoors · Lock the brightness

Overcast days

Don't write off cloudy days. Overcast light is soft and even with no harsh shadows, which is flattering for architecture and interiors. Skip it for ocean shots where you need blue water.

Midday sun

Harsh overhead sun creates deep shadows and blown-out highlights. If you have to shoot midday, look for open shade, like under an overhang or in the shadow of a building, with the bright scene visible behind your subject.

Indoors

  • Face a window. Natural light from the side or front is far better than overhead hotel lighting.
  • Don't shoot with a bright window behind your subject. It silhouettes them.
  • Turn off overhead fluorescent lights if you can. They cast an unflattering green tint.

Lock the brightness

Phones keep adjusting brightness while you film, so a clip that pans from a dim lobby to a bright window will flicker and pump. To stop it, tap and hold on your subject until you see AE/AF LOCK on an iPhone, or a lock icon on Android. Then drag up or down to make it brighter or darker. It stays put until you tap the screen again.

The Basics06

Audio

A lot of people watch Reels with the sound on. Real sound from the place makes footage feel like being there, so treat it as part of the content.

Sounds worth capturing

  • Pours, fizz, ice in a glass, a cork coming out
  • Sizzle from a pan, a knife on a board, a spoon cracking crème brûlée
  • Waves, rain, birds, wind, water in a fountain
  • Live music, a busy market, a room full of happy chatter

These small, satisfying sounds hold attention on their own. Get close to the source and let them play out.

Keep readingKeep it clean

Keep it clean

  • Let the place do the talking. Stay quiet while you film, and give your companion a heads up.
  • If you're speaking to camera, find a spot away from HVAC, loud music, and crowds.
  • If a bar or shop is playing a song you'd recognize, we may not be able to use that sound on a brand account. Grab a take when it's quieter too.
Wind, waves, and a bit of background noise are fine. Imperfect sound feels real. If something sounds great, film it even if the picture isn't perfect.

The Basics07Biggest impact

People in frame

An empty pool is a brochure. Someone lowering into it is a holiday. People are what turn a nice place into a place the viewer can picture themselves in, so film them on purpose.

Why people matter

We are wired to look at other humans. Eyes go to faces, hands, and movement before anything else in the frame, and when someone in a shot looks at something, the viewer looks at it too. On social, posts with people in them consistently earn more likes and comments than posts without. A beautiful space with no one in it is easy to scroll past.

The goal is not a model. It is a person who makes the moment feel lived in. Your travel companion, a bartender, a guide, or you.

Shots that work

  • The walk-in: someone walking away from camera into a view, a lobby, or down a jetty. The viewer follows them in.
  • The look: someone at a railing or window taking in the view. Let their gaze lead the viewer to it.
  • The first reaction: the moment someone sees the room, the suite, or the sunset. Start rolling before they turn the corner.
  • Hands in frame: a glass picked up, a menu opened, a towel unfolded, a key card tapped. Instant point of view, no face required.
  • Scale: one person at a lookout, in a cathedral, or on an empty beach. One person makes a big place feel big.
  • Doing, not posing: swimming, eating, reading, clinking glasses. Natural activity beats looking at the camera.

The people who make the place

Staff and locals are some of the best content you can capture. A chef plating, a bartender building a drink, a guide mid-story, a vendor arranging fruit. They show the expertise and the welcome behind the trip. Ask first. Most people are flattered.

You on camera

You are the most available person on any trip, so use yourself. You don't need to talk. Stand in frame for scale, walk into a view, or sit at a table facing out. If you do speak, keep it to one or two natural sentences of observation, not a sales pitch. "This is the kind of place you can immediately settle into."

It doesn't need to look polished. Natural, phone-shot video from a real person tends to feel more trustworthy than anything that looks like an ad.

Travelling with someone? Hand them the phone. A few seconds of you arriving somewhere is worth more than another empty establishing shot.

Asking permission

  • Background crowds at a resort, market, or street are generally fine.
  • If someone is clearly the subject of the shot, ask before you film. A quick "mind if I film this?" usually does it.
  • Never film children in a way that makes them identifiable without explicit parental consent.
  • If a property restricts filming guests, respect it and let the marketing team know.
  • Unsure? Shoot it both ways: one take with the person, one without.

By experience08

Hotels & accommodations

Hotel content is about scale and atmosphere, helping the viewer understand how a space feels, not just how it looks.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Walk into the lobby with the camera already rolling.
  2. Film the room from the doorway, then open the curtains on camera.
  3. Hold the view from the window or balcony for 30 seconds.
See the full shot listArrival & lobby · Rooms & suites · Pool, beach & common areas

Arrival & lobby

  • Capture the arrival moment: your first impression of the entrance or lobby as you walk in
  • Walk slowly through the lobby, letting the space reveal itself
  • Look for ceiling height, natural light, and architectural details worth lingering on

Rooms & suites

  • Shoot from the doorway first: a wide establishing shot of the room before you touch anything
  • Film the curtains opening. Light flooding a room is one of the best reveals you can get.
  • Capture the view from the window or balcony as its own clip
  • Details worth capturing: interesting bedding, bathroom fixtures, a welcome amenity, a well-placed reading chair
  • Show the room being enjoyed: someone flopping onto the bed, stepping onto the balcony, running a bath

Pool, beach & common areas

  • Golden hour is especially valuable here. Shoot the pool at sunset if you can.
  • Someone swimming, sliding in, or stretching out on a lounger turns a nice pool into a place the viewer wants to be
  • Use foreground interest: a drink on a lounger, towels, an umbrella
  • Capture the scale of the space. Pull back far enough that the viewer understands the full environment
Shoot the room before you unpack. Once your luggage is out, the shot is gone.

By experience09

Restaurants & dining

Food and dining content works best when it's slow, elegant, and never rushed. The goal is to make the viewer wish they were sitting at that table.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Film the dish straight down from above, before anyone touches it.
  2. Catch a slow pour or the first cut into something.
  3. Get one wide shot of the table and the room for context.
See the full shot listThe space · Food & drinks · Combining clips · What to avoid

The space

  • Get the room twice: empty before service with the tables set, and in full swing with guests, clinking glasses, and staff moving through
  • Walk slowly through the dining room as a long, continuous clip
  • Bar setup before guests arrive: bottles, glassware, clean lines
  • The kitchen at work if they'll let you: a chef plating, flames, a pass full of dishes

Food & drinks

The camera eats first. Film the dish before anyone touches it. Once the food is disturbed, the styled shot is gone.

The ideal food clip is long and unhurried: a slow pan across the table, a wide shot of the dining room with the meal in the foreground, a pour that takes its time. Where a longer clip isn't practical, shorter detail shots fill the gap.

  • A straight-down shot from directly above the plate (often called a "flat lay") works particularly well for plated dishes
  • One slow pan across the dish, once, not back and forth
  • Steam rising from a dish or condensation on a glass
  • Pouring wine, coffee, or cocktails (steady hands, slow pour)
  • Simple interactions: a fork breaking a runny egg, a spoon cracking crème brûlée, slicing into a dessert
  • A natural reaction after tasting. Not performed, just genuine

Combining clips

Food often benefits from multiple clips rather than one long shot. Capture both close detail shots and at least one wider shot showing the table and restaurant for context. The detail and the "where" together are stronger than either alone.

What to avoid

  • Overly close shots that lose context
  • Fast pans across dishes
  • Flash or harsh artificial lighting on food
  • Filming people chewing. A bite and a natural reaction is fine, sustained chewing isn't

By experience10

Shopping

Shopping content combines atmosphere with interaction. The goal is to make the viewer feel curious and drawn in, like they're browsing alongside you.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Stand still inside while people browse around you.
  2. Film hands lifting, turning, or opening an item.
  3. Catch the light coming through the storefront window.
See the full shot listThe space · Hands & interaction · What to be mindful of

The space

  • Stand still inside a shop as people browse around you and let the atmosphere do the work
  • Slow walk through an aisle or along a display wall
  • Light coming through storefront windows
  • Shelves, racks, or market tables arranged with care

Hands & interaction

If a longer atmospheric clip isn't possible, shorter detail shots work well here. Hands bring retail spaces to life. Use your own or a companion's to show scale, texture, and quality.

  • Lifting an item from a shelf
  • Flipping through books, records, prints, or textiles
  • Running fingers across fabric, leather, or a carved surface
  • Opening a box, drawer, or case
  • Turning an object over to show a detail or maker's mark
Move slowly. Let the object stay in frame long enough to be understood. A half-second glance at something beautiful is worse than not filming it at all.

What to be mindful of

  • Be respectful of staff and other shoppers
  • Avoid filming price tags unless directly relevant
  • Ask permission in intimate or private spaces

By experience11

Cruise ships

A ship is a hotel, a dozen restaurants, a spa, a theatre and a new view every morning, all on the water. Film the parts only a ship has, and use the other chapters for everything else.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Film your cabin from the doorway, then slide open the balcony door.
  2. Stand at the railing and let the ocean, or the wake, move past.
  3. At sail-away, hold on the port as it slowly falls behind.
Onboard, the rest of this guide still applies. For the cabin, restaurants, bars, spa and shows, use the Hotels, Restaurants, Bars, Spas and Evening entertainment chapters.
See the full shot listYour cabin · At sea · Around the ship · Sail-away & arrivals

Your cabin

The cabin is the question clients ask about most, so film it on day one, before you unpack.

  • One slow walk-in from the door to the window or balcony, without stopping
  • Slide the balcony door open on camera and step out to the view
  • The details people ask about: the closet, the bathroom and shower, where the bags go
  • If you can, peek at a few other cabin types too. Clients compare them.

At sea

  • Stand at the railing with the ocean moving past. Let the water and horizon do the work
  • The wake trailing behind the ship, from the back deck
  • Breakfast or coffee on the balcony with nothing but sea behind it
  • A sunrise or sunset from the top deck. Sea days give you both.

Around the ship

  • Walk slowly along the promenade deck
  • Stand still in the atrium or main lounge to capture the scale
  • The pool deck both ways: calm early in the morning, and lively in the afternoon
  • The observation lounge or any spot with big windows facing forward
  • The ship's signature thing, whatever it is: a glass-walled lounge, a stern infinity pool, a planetarium. If clients would ask "what's special about this ship?", film that.
  • Glass elevators or staircases in motion

Sail-away & arrivals

  • Ship pulling away from port, dock or skyline slowly receding
  • First view of a new port coming into focus as you approach
  • Scenic cruising through fjords, glaciers or a narrow channel. Head to the front of the ship early, before the crowd.
Sail-away moments are some of the most emotive clips you can capture. Be on deck early with your phone ready. They last minutes and don't repeat.

Destinations & touring

The goal is to capture a sense of place: the feeling of being somewhere specific, not just anywhere warm.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Stand still on a street corner and let the scene unfold.
  2. Turn a corner into a view with the camera already rolling.
  3. Have someone walk into the scene from behind the camera.
See the full shot listStreets & daily life · Nature & landscapes · Arrival moments

Streets & daily life

  • Stand still at a street corner and let the scene unfold around you
  • Café scenes from a fixed seat: people, movement, atmosphere
  • Pedestrian streets or markets with natural foot traffic

Nature & landscapes

  • Waves rolling in and out. Hold the shot long enough for two or three full cycles
  • Wind through trees, grass, or fabric
  • Clouds moving across mountains or a skyline
  • Any scene where the light is doing something worth capturing

Arrival moments

  • Turning a corner to reveal a view. Keep the camera rolling as you approach
  • Standing still at a lookout or landmark, letting the scale of it register on camera

By experience13

Spas & wellness

Spa content lives or dies on atmosphere. These are spaces designed to slow people down, and your footage should feel the same way. Quiet, unhurried, and sensory.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Ask the front desk first, then film the reception: flowers, candles, or water.
  2. Get close on tea, oils, stones, or a folded towel.
  3. Hold a still shot of the relaxation lounge or its view.
See the full shot listThe space · Details · What to avoid

The space

  • Reception areas with fresh flowers, candles, or water features make strong establishing shots
  • Treatment corridors: soft lighting, towels folded on a trolley, a half-open door
  • Relaxation lounges: robes draped over chairs, a view through a window, steam rising from a tea service
  • Outdoor thermal pools, rooftop cabanas, or garden settings, especially in good light

Details

  • Product displays: oils, stones, botanicals arranged with care
  • Hands near a treatment table or pressing into a folded towel for tactile detail
  • A cup of herbal tea on a stone surface with a candle nearby
  • Water features: a slow pour, a surface shimmer, steam through glass

What to avoid

  • Never film staff or other guests in treatment areas without explicit permission
  • Avoid filming changing rooms, lockers, or any private areas
  • Ask front desk before filming. Most high-end spas have photography policies
The mood you're capturing is the one your client wants to step into. If the footage feels calm and indulgent, it's working.

Cocktail bars & lounges

The bar is where the evening starts. Great bar content captures the ritual and the atmosphere, not just the drink.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Film a cocktail being built or poured.
  2. Hold on the finished drink as light catches the glass.
  3. Catch a natural toast, two glasses meeting.
See the full shot listThe space · The drink · Timing

The space

  • The bar before it fills up: bottles backlit, glasses lined up, a bartender preparing
  • A slow pan along the bar top from one end to the other
  • Lounge seating with low light, a candle, and a view. Let the ambiance breathe
  • Ship bars with a port view or sea backdrop. The setting is half the content

The drink

  • A cocktail being built: pouring, muddling, a garnish being placed
  • The finished drink on a surface, condensation forming, light catching the glass
  • A champagne pour: bubbles rising, the overflow moment
  • Two glasses raised in a toast, natural, not posed

Timing

Shoot bars at two moments: just before opening when the space is pristine, and early in the evening when the room is alive but not chaotic. Both tell different stories.

Condensation on a cold glass in warm light is one of the most effective short clips you can capture. It reads as luxury without trying.

Shore excursions & cultural touring

Capture the feeling of being a traveller in a place, not a tourist ticking off a list.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Film the tender or the first view as you arrive into port.
  2. Stand still at the landmark and let people move around you.
  3. Find local life: a vendor, a café table, someone at work on their craft.
See the full shot listArrival & first impressions · Cultural sites & landmarks · Local life

Arrival & first impressions

  • The tender or water taxi approaching a dock. Shoot from on board as you near shore
  • The first view of a port town or village as you disembark
  • Walking through a market entrance or city gate for the first time

Cultural sites & landmarks

  • Stand still at a significant landmark and let people move around you. Don't rush to get the clean shot
  • Architectural details: carved stone, coloured tiles, ironwork, painted facades
  • Guided tour moments: a guide gesturing toward something, a small group gathered in a courtyard
  • Religious or historical sites: approach slowly, respect any filming restrictions, prioritise atmosphere over documentation

Local life

  • Street vendors, flower stalls, fishing boats, the texture of a place
  • Café culture: someone reading at an outdoor table, a coffee placed down, pigeons on a square
  • Craftsmanship: a potter at a wheel, a weaver at a loom, a baker pulling bread
Be respectful and observational. Film daily life as you encounter it naturally. Don't direct or stage. Ask permission if you want to film someone closely.

By experience16

Scenery & nature

Nature doesn't need help. It just needs patience and the right moment. The best scenic footage happens when you stop moving and let the world do something.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Find the highest vantage point and hold one long, still shot.
  2. Film water in motion for 30 seconds: a breaking wave, a waterfall.
  3. Put one person in frame for scale.
See the full shot listLandscapes & viewpoints · Water · Light & atmosphere

Landscapes & viewpoints

  • Find the highest or widest vantage point available and hold a long, still shot
  • Include a foreground element (a railing, a path, a wildflower) to give the scale context
  • Panoramic settings with cloud movement or shifting light are worth waiting for

Water

  • Ocean from a cliff or elevated position. Wait for a wave to break
  • Waterfalls: hold the shot for a full 30 seconds minimum, the motion is the content
  • Harbour or lake reflections in calm conditions. Morning is best before wind arrives
  • A river winding through a valley shot from above

Light & atmosphere

  • Sunrise mist over a valley or bay. Set an alarm, it's worth it
  • Last light hitting a mountain range or coastline
  • Rays of light through forest canopy or a stone archway
With scenic content, the temptation is to pan too quickly across a wide view. Resist it. Hold the shot. Let the viewer settle into the landscape. A 45-second still shot of a great view outperforms a frantic 10-second pan every time.

Arrivals & transfers

For luxury travel, how you get there is part of the story. These clips perform well because they trigger anticipation. The viewer wants to be in that car, on that tender, walking through that entrance.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Film the road or water ahead through the window.
  2. Catch the door opening or the first step off.
  3. Film the welcome: a drink offered, a greeter, the first step inside.
See the full shot listThe vehicle · Water & air arrivals · The welcome

The vehicle

  • Interior of a private car or transfer vehicle: leather seats, a chilled water bottle, the view through the window
  • Door being opened by a driver or concierge, filmed from inside as it opens
  • The road ahead through the windscreen: a coastal highway, a mountain pass, a palm-lined drive

Water & air arrivals

  • Seaplane or helicopter approaching a destination. Film through the window as you descend
  • Private yacht or tender arriving at a dock. Shoot from the bow as you approach
  • The moment of stepping off onto a jetty or gangway

The welcome

  • A property entrance: gates opening, a tree-lined driveway, a uniformed greeter
  • The first step inside, shot from behind or to the side as someone walks in
  • A welcome gesture: a champagne glass offered, a cold towel presented, a lei placed
These moments are brief and unrepeatable. Keep your phone accessible during any arrival. You won't get a second take on a property entrance or a tender docking at a remote island.

By experience18

Scenic rail journeys

Train journeys are made for Reels. You have a moving frame, passing landscapes, and a built-in sense of travel and time. Your job is to hold the phone steady and let the scenery do the work.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Wipe the window, then hold one long shot of the landscape passing.
  2. Put a drink on the tray with the scenery behind it.
  3. Keep rolling through a tunnel exit or a bridge crossing.
See the full shot listFrom the window · On board · Exteriors

From the window

  • A long, continuous shot of landscape passing: mountains, valleys, coastline, vineyards
  • Tunnels entering and exiting: a brief darkness and then a reveal
  • A viaduct or bridge. Film looking forward or backward along the train
  • A station platform arriving and departing: the pause and then the pull away

On board

  • The observation or dome car: wide windows, other passengers taking it in
  • White tablecloth dining with scenery rolling past the window behind the table
  • A coffee or glass of wine on the fold-down tray with the landscape reflected in it
  • Details of the carriage: marquetry, brass fittings, a conductor's uniform

Exteriors

If there's an opportunity to film the train from outside (at a station, a crossing, or a hillside vantage point), take it. The scale of a train in a landscape is striking and hard to replicate from inside.

The window shot is the signature of scenic rail content. Clean the glass with a sleeve before you start filming. Streaks and smudges read badly on screen.

By experience19

Evening entertainment

Whether a casino floor, a production show, or a live performance, the goal isn't documentation, it's making the viewer feel like they're missing something.

⏱️ If you only have 2 minutes

  1. Get a wide shot of the room from the back.
  2. Catch the lights going down as the show starts.
  3. Film one detail: a dealer's hands, the roulette wheel, a glass at the bar.
See the full shot listCasino floors · Production shows & live performance · Ambient evening atmosphere

Casino floors

  • Wide establishing shot of the floor: the lights, the movement, the scale
  • A slow drift along a row of machines or tables. Keep moving, don't stop
  • Dealer hands shuffling or cutting cards: close detail, 10–15 seconds
  • The roulette wheel spinning. Get low and close, let the ball do the work
Many casinos restrict filming at tables. Check with staff before you start. Floor-level wide shots are generally fine; close filming of specific players is not.

Production shows & live performance

  • Film from the back or sides of the venue. Never with flash, never standing up
  • Opening moments of a show: the lights going down, the stage lighting up
  • A wide shot of the audience from the back. The scale of a full theatre is compelling
  • Atmosphere clips between acts: the buzz of a crowd, a bar at intermission
Most cruise ship production shows allow short clips from designated areas. Respect the performers, keep your screen brightness down, and keep clips brief.

Ambient evening atmosphere

  • The ship or resort at night: lit pools, glowing windows, the sky
  • An outdoor terrace with fairy lights and a distant horizon
  • The bar in full swing: a wide shot of a busy, well-lit room from the edge
Evening footage is harder to shoot well because of low light. Stay close to light sources, keep your movement slow, and accept that some of it won't be usable. One good evening clip is worth ten grainy ones.

Quick fixes on location

  • Too dark? Move toward a window or a light source, and keep the flash off. Low light looks grainy, not moody.
  • Shot it horizontal? Shoot it again vertically. It's the one mistake editing can't rescue.
  • Shaky? Hold the phone with two hands, elbows tucked in, or lean against a wall or railing.
  • Windy? Keep filming. A bit of wind sounds like being there.
  • Looks hazy or soft? Wipe the lens with a soft cloth or your shirt. Pockets leave a film on it that makes everything look foggy.
  • Brightness keeps changing? Tap and hold on your subject to lock it.
  • Phone full? 4K uses a lot of space. Drop to 1080p rather than missing the moment.
